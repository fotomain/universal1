KPI inside projects: the practices that work

22 practical cases of how key performance indicators are put into projects and project-like work: 7 from the book by K. V. Shchiborsh (Budgeting of the activity of industrial enterprises of Russia, 2001; the BUDGET1 demo project) and 15 from the practice of construction, space, government, pharma, software, oil and consulting. Each case is one paragraph and one row of the table. The last sections say which industries need project KPIs most, what the cases have in common, the formulas, and what of this Universal 1 already has.

Prepared 2026-11-19. Evidence grades: A peer reviewed paper, government audit or official regulation / data; B book, published case or survey research (method is solid, results are self-reported or illustrative); C practitioner or vendor material. Numbers are quoted as the sources give them; where I could not check a number in the original it says so.

Summary: what the 22 cases teach

1. A KPI belongs to a person who can move it

Shchiborsh builds the whole system on controllable factors (cases 1, 2, 5). NASA and the UK portfolio (cases 8, 9) name a responsible party for every reading.

2. Few KPIs, but they must cover the whole task

Not one number: pairs that cannot be gamed alone - cost with schedule (CPI + SPI, case 8), speed with stability (DORA, case 15), output with the cost of getting it (cases 3, 4).

3. The threshold is fixed before the number arrives

RAG colours with written thresholds (cases 9, 22), buffer zones (case 20), the 60% weekly-completion alarm (case 13). Without it the "watermelon" appears: green outside, red inside (cases 10, 22).

4. Measure the plan against the baseline, not against last year

The bonus base is the favourable deviation from the plan, not growth over the previous period (case 3). Benchmarks (case 12) only compare projects measured the same way.

5. Leading and lagging together

Weekly PPC and buffer burn warn early (cases 13, 20); the money and benefit result confirms late (cases 12, 21). A project that has only lagging KPIs finds out at the end.

6. Link the KPI to a decision or a reward

A KPI nobody acts on is decoration. Bonus rates in equilibrium (case 4), incentive pool for the T5 suppliers (case 11), stage gates in pharma (case 14), OKR grading (case 19).

7. Start small and customise

The engineering-design study began with 30 KPIs and found that standard project KPIs work after adapting them to the company (case 17); the Delphi set was tested on one consultancy project (case 18).

8. Real cases fail on reporting, not arithmetic

Crossrail had no plan detailed enough to track progress until late 2018 (case 10); portfolio ratings and PMI "champions" show that measuring benefits, not only cost and time, is what separates leaders (case 21).

Part A. Seven cases from the book

Source: the electronic Word version of K. V. Shchiborsh, Budgeting of the activity of industrial enterprises of Russia, M.: Delo i Servis, 2001, extracted for the BUDGET1 demo project (documentation/BUDGET1). The book speaks about budgets and responsibility centres; the project meaning is the same: a KPI is a budget line that has an owner and a plan-fact comparison. The money examples in the book are examples, not measured results.

1 Five types of responsibility centre decide what the KPI is industry B

The book classifies every unit of an enterprise by the powers of its manager and gives each type its own measure. A managed cost centre (administrative services where inputs and outputs are hard to measure) keeps within its budget and is judged on service level. A standard cost centre (production workshop, procurement, shipping) is held to the normative level of direct labour, direct materials and variable overhead, and its efficiency is the positive or negative variance from the standard; procurement controls purchase prices inside the fixed volume. A revenue centre (the sales department) maximises revenue inside its budget and has no right to overspend or change prices. A profit centre may change costs and prices and is judged on profit, but takes no investment decisions. An investment centre is judged on the return on invested capital or on residual income, profit less a capital charge. In a project this is the rule for choosing the KPI of each project role: a task owner who controls only hours is held to hours and rate, a project manager with a budget to cost and schedule, a project sponsor to benefit and return.

Lesson. Pick the KPI by decision rights. A manager cannot answer for a number he has no power to move.

2 Only controllable factors enter the report industry B

The book splits the indicators of a centre into those set centrally (for example the physical output) and those set by the centre itself (unit costs); both are "controllable". A third group arises in the centre but is outside its zone - the book's example is the depreciation of a workshop building. The monthly report of the centre holds only controllable items, and the management has power only over the centrally set ones; how the target is reached is the centre's choice (after a first month with profit 2,000 thousand roubles behind plan the task given is the gap, not the method). For a standard cost workshop the book rejects "total production cost" as the bonus indicator, because when the centre is ordered a different output the cost changes and so does the bonus. It proposes two indicators: unit variable costs multiplied by the planned output, and the absolute amount of general production overhead. Where the place of cost and the responsible centre differ, internal transfer prices at planned unit cost move the variance to the supplier workshop.

Lesson. When the plan of volume changes during the period the KPI must be recalculated on the plan volume, otherwise it measures the planner, not the performer.

3 Reward the favourable deviation; separate "condition" from "indicator" industry B

Principle 3 of the book: the base of the bonus is the favourable deviation from the plan, not the growth over the previous period. Meeting the plan exactly earns zero bonus because the salary already pays for it; the bonus fund starts when the budget task is overfulfilled. The book states that most Russian plants did the opposite - bonuses for "1% lower cost" or "1% more sales" against the previous period. Principle 4 then separates two kinds of parameter. A condition is a parameter set from above that must be met exactly, with no plus and no minus. An indicator is a parameter with a limit value where the target is to do better. The example: overfulfilling the output plan of a foundry workshop is harmful, because it burns materials for blanks that have neither processing capacity nor market demand, so output is a condition and the bonus is built on cost savings. A second mistake the book names is an incomplete list of indicators: a branch that decides its own short-term credit and payment terms needs indicators for liquidity too, otherwise it will optimise the visible ones.

Lesson. Mark each project KPI as "must hit" (a scope or quality gate) or "more is better" (cost saving, early finish). Rewarding a must-hit number for overshoot creates waste.

4 Bonus rates in equilibrium industry B

When a unit has two or more indicators, the book requires rates that pay the same for one rouble of extra company profit. Worked example: the sales department earns 10% of revenue above plan and also a bonus for saving its operating-expense estimate. Product N sells at 100 per unit with planned variable cost 80. One extra unit gives revenue +100 and gross profit +20, and a 10% bonus on revenue pays 10, so 0.5 of bonus per 1 of profit. A saving of 1 rouble of the expense estimate is exactly 1 rouble of profit, so the rate for savings must also be 0.5 per rouble. Otherwise the manager picks the easier way to his bonus ("moral hazard"). Principle 7 extends this across divisions: the book computes an average company bonus rate below 1, then prices each division's indicators so that equal profit contribution gives equal bonus, in the example an oil production unit and a refinery of a vertically integrated company. Principle 9 adds that the choice of base changes rarely but the coefficient is revised every short budget period.

Lesson. If KPIs carry money, compute the rate from the profit effect of each, and test the pairs for "cheaper way to earn" before launching.

5 Internal arbitration keeps the KPI fair industry B

Principle 5: a person is rewarded only for what he controls. The book's tool is the internal arbitration. After the production task is done, the actual cost of a unit's gross output is corrected by internal fines and claims between units, branches and subsidiaries: received claims add, paid claims subtract. A claim can be raised for non-delivery of raw material, poor quality, or broken delivery rhythm. The quality control department or a special arbitration commission examines it and writes an act with the money damage and the plan shortfall; the damage is put on the side found guilty. A unit may also go to the commission when an external supplier fails. The corrected "cost-accounting" cost of the centre is then the base of its bonus.

Lesson. A project with dependent teams needs a written rule for who carries a delay caused by a predecessor, or every delay report becomes an argument.

6 Plan-fact analysis in three stages, reports at three levels industry B

Control in the book is monitoring during the period and analysis after it. During the period, centres report monthly for a quarterly budget, management services collect them into summary operating reports and make recommendations to the person responsible for the budget. The report of a centre has three levels: totals by item, then the explanation of each significant item (called disclosures in the book, for example for a workshop), and the supporting data. After the period the analysis has three stages. Stage 1 compares plan and fact of the output reports: profit and loss, cash flow, investments, financial state. Stage 2 analyses the operating, investment and financial sub-budgets by factors (price against volume, for example). Stage 3 is the synthesis: the sub-budgets are linked through cash movement, so the conclusions are combined into corrections for the next period. The book does the example "as an outsider", because insiders know too much to see the figures cleanly, and uses the result for two aims: planning of the next period and control and incentive by centre.

Lesson. Report exceptions top-down with drill-down; always split a variance into factors so that the owner of the factor can act.

7 Two horizons: staff by quarter, managers by year industry B

Principle 8: the base of bonus calculation is the same for staff and manager of a centre (given by the centre type), but the time frame differs. Staff are rewarded for overfulfilling short-term indicators, the quarterly budget, because turnover among ordinary staff is higher. The manager's bonus partly follows the short budget, but its main part follows the long "development budget", for example for a year, because managers are the core of the firm and should care about the long result. Together with principle 9 (flexible coefficients), this is the book's answer to "how often do we change the KPI": the list of parameters stays, the weights are revised each short period.

Lesson. In a long project give the team sprint-level KPIs and give the project manager stage and benefit KPIs, so that nobody is paid to shift cost to the next stage.

Part B. Fifteen cases from practice

Found by web research on 2026-11-19. Where the search only returned a summary, the paragraph says what could not be checked. The vendor "worked examples" of SPI and CPI found on the way are shown in the formulas section, not as cases, because they are illustrations.

8 NASA: a money threshold switches on Earned Value aerospace, defence A

The NASA FAR Supplement requires an Earned Value Management System on development or production work, including flight and ground support and institutional projects. For cost-type or fixed-price-incentive contracts of $50 million and more the system must be found compliant by the cognizant federal agency; from $20 million to under $50 million it must comply with ANSI/EIA-748; below $20 million, EVM is optional and a risk-based decision of the project manager. Firm-fixed-price work is a special case: EVM is discouraged, but an Integrated Master Schedule and schedule reporting are still required from $20 million. The contract requirements checklist puts the monthly EVMS report into the acquisition package as a deliverable. The sources I read give the rule, not the outcome; the CPI / SPI limits are in NPR 7120.5, which I did not read.

Lesson. Scale the KPI machine to the size and risk of the work; a small task does not need a full EVMS, a large one cannot go without it.

9 UK major projects: independent RAG rating government A

The UK central authority (the Infrastructure and Projects Authority, now merged into NISTA) rates every project of the Government Major Projects Portfolio by a Delivery Confidence Assessment, "the rating of a project's chance of success at a specific point in time". Since 2022 the scale is red, amber, green, tied to a stage gate assessment. The authority publishes an annual report and project-level data with the rating of the authority and of the senior responsible owner, and department commentary. The first NISTA report (2024-25) counted 135 amber, 31 red and 30 green. The value for a project office is the structure: two independent readings of the same project, a fixed scale, a fixed date, and a public record.

Lesson. Give the portfolio one rating scale with written meaning and let someone other than the project manager read it.

10 Crossrail: no baseline, no KPI rail megaproject A failure

The UK National Audit Office found that Crossrail Ltd did not start to produce a delivery plan detailed enough to track progress until late 2018, and that the revised schedule and budget agreed in April 2019 were unachievable because the programme was further from complete than the company and its sponsors understood. The 2019 report named the compressed schedule, the contract model, lack of pressure on costs and the absence of an achievable plan among the causes. In late 2018 the sponsors appointed a new chair and chief executive. The 2021 follow-up gave a forecast cost to complete of £18.9bn (including Network Rail costs, excluding new trains and depot) and said the cost and schedule were beyond the budget available. I did not read the lessons appendix in full, so the lessons here are the audit's findings and not a list of KPIs.

Lesson. Percent-complete and CPI / SPI are only as good as the baseline plan they are measured against; build the detailed plan first and re-baseline openly.

11 Heathrow Terminal 5: one agreement, one incentive pool airport construction B

For T5 the owner BAA used the T5 Agreement. In the sources I read, the owner kept the risk of the project (unlike a normal cost-incentive contract, where risk is shared with suppliers), and the suppliers worked as one integrated team with an incentive fund that rewarded completion below the target budget and good safety, time and quality performance. The case is studied at Manchester (teaching case "BAA: The T5 Project Agreement") and in papers by Brady and Davies. The search did not return the actual KPI definitions, weights or the pain-gain ratio, so those cannot be quoted; what the case shows is the design: several KPI families, a joint pool, and no cost-only reward.

Lesson. A reward built on cost alone pushes quality and safety down; put all four families into the incentive.

12 CII benchmarking: the same KPI for hundreds of projects construction, EPC A

The Construction Industry Institute started its Benchmarking and Metrics programme in 1995 so that member companies can compare their finished projects with a common database and measure the value of best practices. Metrics are cost growth and schedule growth, overall and by project phase, with safety (recordable incident rate and lost-workday-case rate), changes and rework. One study counted 395 projects worth nearly $21 billion; researchers use the data to test contract incentives, constructability and delivery systems against cost growth, schedule growth and safety. Scoring ranges for the metrics are derived from the statistical distribution of the database. CII also states that benchmarking and best practices bring up to 18% cost improvement (a figure from the CII site that I did not check against its study). Today the site lists a corporate safety benchmark and a best-practice implementation benchmark.

Lesson. A KPI definition has to be written down in detail (what counts as cost growth, from which baseline), otherwise project data cannot be compared.

13 Last Planner: the weekly Percent Plan Complete building construction B

In the Last Planner System the foremen and trade contractors agree the work of the coming week, and the KPI is PPC: tasks completed as planned divided by tasks planned. Every missed task gets a reason, so the project learns why plans fail. A mixed-methods study by Power, Sinnott and Lynch (2021) cites a finding that less than 60% of planned tasks are executed weekly in many projects, and concludes that a competent LPS facilitator embedded on the project helps implementation; an Irish study of two capital projects (Power and Taylor, 2019) used PPC with reasons for non-completion. The warnings are as important as the benefits: Ballard and Tommelein caution against over-focus on PPC, since a project may show a high PPC and still fall behind its schedule, and the effect on productivity is suggestive, not established, in the sources I found (mostly single project studies, some of them student theses).

Lesson. Use PPC as the weekly early signal and the reason codes as the improvement list, but check it against the milestone plan.

14 AstraZeneca: the KPI is the quality of the gate decision pharma R&D A

Cook and colleagues (2014) reviewed AstraZeneca's small-molecule projects of 2005-2010 and derived five determinants of success - the right target, right tissue, right safety, right patient and right commercial potential - and proposed that every project is examined on them. A follow-up (Morgan et al., 2018) reports that the success rate from candidate nomination to phase III completion rose from 4% in 2005-2010 to 19% in 2012-2016. The correction matters: the 4%-to-19% figure comes from the 2018 paper, the 2014 paper itself said it was too early to prove an effect, and the two numbers measure different stages from the "2% vs 6% to market" often quoted. The KPI of the portfolio is the success rate per stage, and of the project the evidence behind each of the five questions at each gate.

Lesson. For research and uncertain work, measure the quality of go / no-go decisions and the attrition per stage; schedule and cost indices say little.

15 DORA: four metrics that watch speed and stability together software B

The DORA research programme (Google Cloud, State of DevOps reports) uses four measures of software delivery: deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate and time to restore service. Teams are grouped into performance clusters; the 2021 report gives for the top group several deployments a day, lead time under an hour and restoration under an hour, while low performers restore in a week to a month. The grouping changed over the years (three clusters in 2022, four in 2024), so benchmarks should be cited with the year. The data come from the pipeline and the incident tool, which is why this KPI set is hard to fake and cheap to collect; the results of the studies are survey based and self-reported.

Lesson. Pair a throughput KPI with a quality KPI, collect them automatically, and benchmark against the year of the report.

16 Mobil NAM&R: one scorecard cascaded to units oil, downstream B

Kaplan and Norton call Mobil's North America Marketing and Refining unit "perhaps our best example" of the strategy-focused organisation. The Balanced Scorecard project started in 1994, a warm winter in 1995 cut heating oil and gas sales, and the unit moved from centrally controlled to decentralised business units, each with its own scorecard (financial, customer, internal process, learning), and staff functions with scorecards whose customers were internal units. The productivity strategy is credited with a 20% reduction in the cost of refining, marketing and delivering a gallon of gasoline. These details come from secondary summaries and the abstract of the Harvard case 197-120; the full case and the book The Strategy-Focused Organization are the primary sources.

Lesson. A portfolio or programme KPI needs a strategy map from top goals to unit measures, with staff functions also measured by their internal customers.

17 KPI reporting for engineering design at an equipment manufacturer engineering A

Gries and Restrepo (ICED 2011) ran a 15-month case study in three locations of a power generation equipment manufacturer to build a KPI reporting concept for design projects and a management dashboard. The team began with a long list of 30 engineering KPIs, drawn partly from the staff, and concluded that standard project management KPIs fit engineering design work after customisation to the nature and size of the company, projects and teams. They also saw that the engineers preferred to manage by the KPIs the management had chosen. The authors say they cannot yet propose a fixed set of design-specific KPIs and that company culture and the consulting approach may have shaped the result. I read the abstract and excerpts, not the full paper.

Lesson. Do not invent KPIs for knowledge work; start from the standard set, cut the long list with management, and expect to re-tune.

18 A KPI set chosen by experts and tested on a consulting project consulting A

Montero, Onieva and Palacin (2015) selected a comprehensive set of project-management KPIs by the Delphi method with a panel of experts and validated its flexibility and customisation on a consultancy project; a later study applied the indicators for control on different project types, with a pilot on a five-year strategic consulting project. The point for practice is the procedure: expert panel chooses once, the project manager picks from the set, the set is adapted per project type. I only saw the abstracts and metadata, so the indicator list itself is not reproduced here.

Lesson. Keep a company-wide KPI catalogue and let each project select its subset, instead of inventing a new list per project.

19 OKR: few public goals, graded technology B

OKRs came from Andy Grove's system at Intel, built on Peter Drucker's management by objectives; John Doerr learned it at Intel in the 1970s and brought it to Google in 1999, when the company had about 40 people. The structure is three to five objectives, each with measurable key results, set openly each quarter. A commonly cited practice is that stretch OKRs are graded and an average of 0.6-0.7 is healthy; I found this only in a secondary blog, not in Google's or Doerr's own text. For projects the use is natural where the baseline moves (product work, agile teams) and a fixed EVM baseline would be rewritten every month.

Lesson. Where the plan is fluid, measure the outcome (key result) and keep the goal list short and visible.

20 Critical chain: watch how fast the buffer burns ETO manufacturing, shipbuilding C

Goldratt's critical chain method removes the safety time from tasks and puts it into a project buffer at the end and feeding buffers where side chains join the critical chain. The KPI is the fever chart: percent of the project buffer consumed against percent of the critical chain completed, with green, yellow and red zones; in a sample in the sources, 55% of the chain was complete with 40% of the buffer used, which reads as slightly ahead. Consultants report lead-time reductions of 25-50%, but those figures come from advocates, not from independent studies, and an academic critique exists (Raz, Barnes and Dvir, 2003). Treat it as a good early-warning chart rather than a proven productivity method.

Lesson. A relative indicator (buffer used against progress) warns before the end date moves, and needs only remaining duration from owners.

21 PMI: leaders measure the benefit, not only cost and time all industries B

PMI's yearly Pulse of the Profession surveys show how organisations measure project success. In 2013 less than two thirds of projects met goals and business intent, and about $135 million was wasted per $1 billion invested; by 2017 the success rate was 69% and waste $97 per $1,000, and for the first time success included benefits realization, where only 31% rated their maturity high or very high. The 2018 report defines "champions" as organisations completing 80% and more of their projects on time, on budget, meeting business intent, with high benefits realization maturity: 92% average success against 32% for underperformers, and 1.4% against 29.1% of money wasted. These are survey results and a classification made by PMI, not proof of cause, but they agree with case 14 and the portfolio view in case 9.

Lesson. Add a benefit KPI (money or outcome) to the cost-time pair and keep an owner for it after the project closes.

22 Watermelon reporting and written RAG thresholds IT, any programme C anti-pattern

A "watermelon" project is green outside and red inside: the status report is green while bugs, delays and lack of progress pile up. The practitioner articles agree on the causes: not fraud but a culture in which a red status brings blame and not help, and optimism in a flat organisation where nobody wants to report the slip. The cure they give is the same everywhere: write down in advance what makes a status amber or red and when it has to change, so that the colour is a fact and not a mood. Cases 9 and 10 show the same problem at the scale of a nation: an independent reading and a detailed baseline are the two safeguards.

Lesson. Define the RAG thresholds on numbers (CPI, SPI, buffer, PPC), compute the colour, and let the PMO check disagreements.

Pitfalls the sources name

  • Bonus on growth over the previous period instead of deviation from the plan (case 3).
  • Total cost as a workshop KPI when the output is set by someone else (case 2).
  • Unequal rates for two ways of earning the same profit (case 4).
  • Too few indicators, so the manager optimises the visible ones (case 3).
  • High PPC and a late project at the same time (case 13).
  • Benchmarks quoted without the year of the report (case 15) or without the stage they measure (case 14).
  • Green status over a failing project (case 22), and a plan too coarse to track (case 10).

Formulas the cases use

Earned Value (cases 8, 10)

PV planned value (budgeted work scheduled) EV earned value (budgeted cost of work done) AC actual cost CV = EV - AC SV = EV - PV CPI = EV / AC SPI = EV / PV EAC = BAC / CPI TCPI = (BAC - EV) / (BAC - AC)

Illustrations from vendor sites, not cases: EV 120,000 and AC 135,000 give CPI 0.89 (over budget); EV 30,000 and PV 36,000 give SPI 0.83 (behind). SPI is unreliable when baselines change often.

PPC and buffer (cases 13, 20)

PPC = tasks completed as planned / tasks planned (week) Buffer used % = buffer consumed / project buffer Chain done % = critical chain completed / critical chain Fever zone = f(buffer used %, chain done %)

Equilibrium bonus rate (case 4)

profit per unit = price - variable cost = 100 - 80 = 20 bonus per unit = price x rate = 100 x 0.10 = 10 rate per profit = 10 / 20 = 0.5 => pay 0.5 per 1 of expense saved

Cost growth, ROI, residual income (cases 1, 12)

cost growth = (final cost - budget) / budget schedule growth = (final duration - planned) / planned ROI = (revenue - cost) / cost residual income = profit - capital charge x capital employed

DORA (case 15)

deployment frequency deploys / day lead time for changes commit -> production change failure rate failed deploys / deploys time to restore incident start -> restored
